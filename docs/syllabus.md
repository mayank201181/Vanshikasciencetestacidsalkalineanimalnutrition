## A. ACIDS & ALKALIS (AA)

### Cover sheet — lessons and objectives
1. **Acids and Alkalis** (Longman p.82)
   - Recall some everyday substances that are acids and alkalis
   - State some common laboratory acids and alkalis
   - Identify acids and alkalis using red and blue litmus paper
   - Recognise common hazards and hazard symbols
   - Describe how to reduce the risk, e.g. acids by dilution
2. **Making Indicators** (p.82–83)
   - Recall examples of everyday substances that are acids and alkalis
   - Recall the colour changes associated with some common indicators (e.g. litmus)
   - Describe how indicators are used to distinguish between acidic, alkaline and neutral solutions
   - Explain how to make an indicator to test different substances (red cabbage indicator at home)
   - Evaluate the effectiveness of different indicators
3. **Universal indicator and the pH scale** (p.82–83)
   - Describe how universal indicator is used to distinguish between acidic, alkaline and neutral solutions, and measure the pH of a solution
   - Describe the pH scale as a numbered scale that shows how acidic or alkaline a solution is
   - Know that solutions below pH 7 are acidic, those above pH 7 are alkaline and those at pH 7 are neutral
   - Describe solutions as being more or less acidic / alkaline by comparing their pH
4. **Neutralisation** (p.87–88)
   - Define a neutralisation reaction as the reaction between an acid and an alkali to produce salt and water
   - Recall the general equation for neutralisation: acid + alkali → salt + water
   - Know how to make a neutral solution from a metal hydroxide and acid, and how to obtain the salt from the neutral solution by evaporation
5. **Making salts**
   - Know how to name salts from the names of acids and alkalis, limited to metal hydroxides reacting with hydrochloric, sulfuric and nitric acids
   - Write word equations for the above reactions, identifying the products and reactants
   - Recall that a base neutralises an acid and that an alkali is a soluble base
6. **Everyday neutralisation**
   - Recall some applications of neutralisation reactions
   - Describe the reactions of acids and bases
   - Explain how everyday examples of neutralisation are useful
7–8. **Investigating indigestion** — KEY ASSESSMENT O1 (Results Table)
9. Revision
10. Progress Check  ← this test

### Knowledge organiser (AA)
Keywords:
- **Acid**: corrosive substance which has a pH lower than 7. Acidity is caused by a high concentration of hydrogen ions.
- **Acidic**: having a pH lower than 7.
- **Alkali**: a base which is soluble in water.
- **Alkaline**: having a pH greater than 7.
- **Base**: a substance that reacts with an acid to neutralise it and produce a salt.
- **Neutralise**: to make neutral by removing any acidic or alkaline nature.
- **Neutral**: when a substance is neither acidic nor alkaline, and has a pH of 7.
- **Litmus paper**: an indicator that can be red or blue. Red litmus turns blue in alkalis, while blue litmus turns red in acids.
- **pH**: a scale of acidity or alkalinity. A pH value below 7 is acidic, a pH value above 7 is alkaline.
- **Universal indicator paper**: paper stained with universal indicator, a chemical solution that produces many different colour changes corresponding to different pH levels.

Acids — "If you look around your kitchen, you may find some acids to eat or drink":
- Vitamin C (oranges) – **ascorbic acid**; Lemons – **citric acid**; Vinegar – **ethanoic acid**; Fizzy drink – **carbonic acid**.
- Some acids are more dangerous. **Hydrochloric acid (HCl)**, **sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄)** and **nitric acid (HNO₃)** are acids used in the science lab. These acids can come as dilute or concentrated.
- Dilute acids are not as dangerous as concentrated acids. This is because there are **fewer acid particles in the same volume**.
- **Irritant** hazard sign (exclamation mark): used for substances that are not corrosive but are irritants. Usually found on more **dilute** acids and alkalis.
- **Corrosive** hazard sign: usually found on more **concentrated** acids and alkalis.

Bases:
- A base is a substance that can react with acids and neutralise them. **Metal oxides, metal hydroxides and metal carbonates** are examples of bases.
- Many bases are **insoluble** — they don't dissolve in water. However, if a base does dissolve in water, we also call it an **alkali**.
- Some alkalis are harmful. However, many are harmless and useful. Many cleaning products such as **bleach, washing powder and oven cleaner** contain alkalis. The most dangerous alkalis in our homes are **oven cleaners** and **caustic soda** (used to unblock drains).
- Soap and washing-up liquid are safe (mild) alkalis. Oven cleaner is a very strong alkali which is very corrosive.

Indicators:
- **Blue litmus** paper turns **red** when it is put into an acid. If the substance was an alkali or neutral, the blue litmus paper would stay blue.
- **Red litmus** paper turns **blue** when it is put into an alkali. If the substance was an acid or neutral, the red litmus paper would stay red.

pH scale (0–14 on the organiser; the glossary says 1–14): red (strongly acidic) → orange → yellow → green (7, neutral) → blue → indigo/purple (strongly alkaline).

Hazard symbols summary (red diamond pictograms):
- Exploding bomb — explosion or reactivity hazards
- Flame — fire hazards (flammable)
- Flame over circle — oxidising hazards
- Gas cylinder — gases under pressure
- Corrosion — corrosive damage to metals, as well as skin and eyes
- Skull and crossbones — can cause death or toxicity with short exposure to small amounts
- Health hazard — may cause or is suspected of causing serious health effects
- Exclamation mark — may cause less serious health effects (irritant/harmful) or damage the ozone layer
- Environment — may cause damage to the aquatic environment
- Biohazard — organisms or toxins that can cause disease in people or animals

### AA glossary (school wording)
- Acid: a substance that turns litmus red. It has a pH of less than 7.
- Alkali: a substance that turns litmus blue. It has a pH of more than 7. A soluble base.
- Antacid: an indigestion remedy that contains a base to neutralise the excess acid in the stomach.
- Base: any substance, soluble or insoluble, that neutralises an acid forming only a salt and water.
- Chemical reaction: a change in which new substances are made.
- Concentrated: a solution that contains a large amount of solute dissolved in a small amount of liquid (solvent).
- Corrosive: a corrosive substance can destroy metals, stonework and skin.
- Diluted: a substance that has had water added to it to make it less concentrated.
- Hazard: something that could cause harm.
- Indicator: a dye that changes to different colours in acids and alkalis.
- Insoluble: describes a substance that cannot be dissolved in a liquid.
- Neutral: a substance that is neither an acid nor an alkali. It has a pH of 7.
- Neutralisation: a reaction in which an acid reacts with an alkali or a base to produce a salt and water only.
- pH scale: a numerical scale from 1 to 14 showing how acidic or alkaline a substance is. Acids have a pH below 7, neutral substances have a pH of 7 and alkalis have a pH greater than 7.
- Precaution: an action taken to reduce the risk of a hazard causing harm (e.g. wearing eye protection when handling an acid to prevent it splashing in your eyes).
- Product: the new substances made in a chemical reaction. These are written on the right side, after the arrow, in a word equation.
- Reactant: the substances that take part in a chemical reaction. These are written on the left side, before the arrow, in a word equation.
- Risk: the chance that a hazard will actually cause harm.
- Salt: the substance (other than water) that is formed when an acid reacts with an alkali or a base.
- Sodium chloride: the chemical name for table or common salt.
- Soluble: describes a substance that can dissolve in a liquid.
- Universal indicator: substance that turns different colours depending on the pH of a solution, and can detect pH across the whole pH range.
- Word equation: an equation in which the names of the reactant(s) are written on the left side, there is an arrow pointing from left to right and the names of the product(s) are written on the right side. Reactants → products.

### Lessons 6–8 background (standard KS3 content the class covers)
- Everyday neutralisation: indigestion tablets/antacids (e.g. magnesium hydroxide, calcium carbonate) neutralise excess hydrochloric acid in the stomach; farmers add lime (calcium hydroxide / calcium oxide / calcium carbonate) to acidic soil; lime added to lakes damaged by acid rain; toothpaste is mildly alkaline and neutralises acids made by bacteria in the mouth (helps prevent tooth decay); bee stings are acidic — traditionally treated with an alkali such as bicarbonate of soda (baking soda) or soap; wasp stings are alkaline — traditionally treated with a weak acid such as vinegar; nettle stings contain an acid.
- Investigating indigestion (Key Assessment O1 — results table): compare antacid remedies by how much acid they neutralise (e.g. add dilute hydrochloric acid with a few drops of universal indicator; add powdered antacid a spatula/measured mass at a time, or add acid drop by drop to a fixed mass of antacid, until neutral/green). Working-scientifically skills: independent variable (what you change — type of antacid), dependent variable (what you measure — e.g. number of drops of acid / mass of antacid needed), control variables (volume and concentration of acid, mass of tablet, same indicator, stirring, temperature); results table with the independent variable in the first column, units in the column headings (not in the cells), repeat readings and a mean; spotting anomalous results; drawing a conclusion.

## B. ANIMAL NUTRITION (AN)

### Knowledge organiser "Digestion" — keywords
- **Digestion**: the breakdown of large insoluble food molecules into smaller soluble molecules.
- **Digestive system**: organ system involved in breaking food down so that it can be absorbed into the bloodstream.
- **Absorbed**: when a substance is taken in by something, or moved across a barrier such as a cell membrane.
- **Amylase**: an enzyme that can break down starch into simple sugars.
- **Lipase**: enzyme that breaks down lipids (fats & oils).
- **Carbohydrase**: enzyme that breaks down carbohydrates.
- **Protease**: enzyme that breaks down proteins.
- **Enzyme**: a protein which catalyses or speeds up a chemical reaction.
- **Surface area**: the area of the surface of an organism or membrane.
- **Villi**: finger-like projections in the small intestine that provide a large surface area for the absorption of food.
- **Capillary**: tiny blood vessels with walls one cell thick where exchange of materials occurs.
- **Bile**: substance produced in the liver. It emulsifies fats to prepare them for digestion.
- **Pancreas**: produces biological catalysts called enzymes which speed up the digestive reactions.
- **Excretion**: process by which waste products from chemical reactions in an organism are removed.

Organ functions:
- Oesophagus: also known as the gullet. Connects the mouth to the stomach. Food is pushed down using contractions of muscles.
- Liver: production of bile.
- Stomach: churns and mixes the food with hydrochloric acid and enzymes.
- Pancreas: produces biological catalysts called enzymes which speed up the digestive reactions.
- Small intestine: absorption of digested food into the bloodstream; production of enzymes to aid digestion.
- Large intestine: absorption of excess water.
- Rectum: storage of faeces (undigested material) before it leaves the body.
- Anus: where faeces leave the body.

Enzymes: "Enzymes are not living things. They are special proteins that can break large molecules into smaller molecules."
- Starch → (carbohydrase) → glucose / sugars; protein → (protease) → amino acids; lipid → (lipase) → fatty acids + glycerol.
- Minerals, vitamins and water are already small enough to be absorbed by the body without being broken down, so they are not digested.
- Digestive enzymes cannot break down dietary fibre, which is why the body cannot absorb it.

Adaptations of the small intestine — adapted for efficient absorption of digested food into the bloodstream by:
- having a very large surface area (villi);
- being surrounded by lots of blood capillaries;
- thin walls (1 cell thick) for faster absorption.

Food tests:
- Starch: drop iodine solution onto the food → turns from orange(-brown) to **blue-black** if starch is present.
- Glucose (sugar): mix chopped food with water, add **Benedict's solution**, heat in a hot water bath → blue changes to green / yellow / orange / **brick red** if sugar is present (brick red = lots of sugar).
- Protein: add **biuret** reagent (potassium hydroxide + copper sulfate) → blue turns **purple / lilac** if protein is present.
- Lipid (fat): shake food with **ethanol**, then pour into water → a **milky white emulsion** (cloudy layer) if fat is present.

Table on the page (typed by the teacher):
| Macromolecule | Broken down into | By enzyme | In location |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate (starch) | Glucose | CARBOHYDRASE: amylase, maltase | Saliva (mouth), small intestine |
| Protein | Amino acids | PROTEASE | Stomach, small intestine |
| Lipids (triglyceride) | Glycerol + 3 fatty acids | LIPASE | Small intestine |
N.B. Enzymes are MADE in the pancreas and released into the small intestine.

### AN word glossary (school wording, with examples)
- Digestion — breakdown of large insoluble food molecules into small soluble ones. e.g. starch in bread is broken down into smaller soluble sugars in the mouth by an enzyme called amylase.
- Carbohydrates — for energy. e.g. bread and pasta give the body energy for activities like running.
- Proteins — for growth and repair. e.g. eating meat helps the body repair and grow muscles after playing sports.
- Fats (lipids) — for stored energy and insulation. e.g. butter gives energy and helps keep the body warm.
- Vitamins — for healthy body maintenance. e.g. vitamin C from oranges helps your body launch an immune response, e.g. against the virus that causes the common cold.
- Minerals — for healthy bones, eyes, hair etc. e.g. calcium in milk keeps your bones and teeth strong.
- Fibre — aids digestion, keeps faeces moving along the digestive system. e.g. wholegrain cereal contains fibre which helps you go to the toilet regularly.
- Starch test — iodine turns blue-black if starch is present. e.g. iodine on a potato turns from orange to blue-black.
- Sugar/glucose test — Benedict's solution changes colour if glucose is present. e.g. heating apple juice with Benedict's turns it brick red, showing a lot of sugar is present.
- Protein test — biuret reagent turns lilac if protein is present. e.g. biuret added to chicken turns from blue to lilac.
- Fat test — ethanol and water form a milky layer if fat is present. e.g. cheese mixed first with ethanol and then with water makes a milky layer.
- Mouth — where digestion begins (mechanical and chemical). e.g. chewing bread mixes it with saliva (which contains amylase) to start digestion of carbohydrates.
- Oesophagus — tube from mouth to stomach. e.g. swallowing sends food down the oesophagus to the stomach.
- Stomach — mixes food with acid and enzymes. e.g. the stomach uses acid to kill bacteria, and the acid creates the right conditions for protease enzymes to break down the protein in meat into amino acids.
- Small intestine — where nutrients are absorbed. e.g. small, soluble glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol are absorbed into the blood here.
- Villi — finger-like structures increasing surface area for absorption. e.g. villi help the small intestine absorb more nutrients in a period of time.
- Liver — makes bile to help digest fats. e.g. the liver makes bile which emulsifies (breaks UP, not down) fat from larger droplets into smaller droplets.
- Pancreas — produces digestive enzymes. e.g. the pancreas makes enzymes which are released into the small intestine to help digest large, insoluble food molecules.
- Large intestine — absorbs water from undigested food. e.g. water is absorbed from undigested food to make solid faeces.
- Rectum — stores waste before it leaves the body.
- Anus — where waste exits the body.
- Enzymes — chemicals that break down food molecules. e.g. amylase breaks starch into sugar (maltose).
- Protease — enzyme that breaks down proteins into amino acids.
- Carbohydrase — enzyme that breaks down large insoluble carbohydrates into small soluble sugars, e.g. glucose.
- Lipase — enzyme that breaks down fats (e.g. in butter) into fatty acids and glycerol.
- Malnutrition — poor diet leading to health problems. e.g. not eating enough protein can make children very weak.
- Dehydration — not enough water in the body. e.g. not drinking water can make you feel tired and give you a headache.
- Obesity — being very overweight, leading to health issues. e.g. eating too much junk food can make you overweight and unhealthy.

### Other AN lessons in the OneNote section (standard KS3 8A content)
- A balanced diet (the seven nutrient groups incl. water and fibre, in the right amounts); reading nutrition information (bar charts / food labels per 100 g); energy needs differ with age, activity, size, pregnancy.
- Deficiency diseases (e.g. scurvy — lack of vitamin C; rickets — lack of vitamin D/calcium; anaemia — lack of iron) — awareness level.
- How enzymes work (lock-and-key idea; enzymes work best at body temperature, ~37 °C) — awareness level.
- Journey of food through the digestive system: mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → rectum → anus. Liver, gall bladder and pancreas add substances but food does not pass through them. Muscles push food along (peristalsis).
- Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder, released into the small intestine; it emulsifies fats (bigger surface area for lipase) and is alkaline, neutralising stomach acid.
